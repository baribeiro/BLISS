Abstract

Machine-learning surrogates are increasingly used to predict how a structure responds to its geometry. However, existing scientific-ML benchmarks rarely use a change in the physical mechanism itself as the source of a distribution shift. Their shifts come from random splits, new initial conditions or held-out ranges of sampled inputs. Here, we introduce BLISS, a dataset and benchmark of 8,528 imperfect hemispherical shells simulated with finite elements until collapse, in which an instability creates the shift. A small change in geometry, two defects moving close enough to interact, changes how the shell collapses. Models map the surface with its defects to three outputs of the collapse, the collapse load (knockdown factor), the displacement field and the failure site where collapse starts. Within distribution, as on existing benchmarks, the best models reach a field R² of 0.985 and find 98% of clear failure sites. With deeper defects they lose the failure site where defects interact. Trained on shells with far-apart defects and tested on shells with close defects, every model that reads the shell geometry scores below a constant prediction of the average training shell on the field. Moving a single pair of defects from 25° to 10° apart lowers the collapse load by 14% in the solver, while the models predict almost no change. Five dense examples recover most of the field of two models, and the choice between two nearly equal failure sites stays close to chance. Our simulations match eight physical tests within 3.3%.

Dataset

Each BLISS sample is one imperfect shell, a clamped hemisphere (R = 25.4 mm, R/t = 110) with small inward defects, loaded by external pressure until it collapses. The input is the surface deviation w at the 76,805 nodes of one shared mesh. The targets are the knockdown factor κ, the displacement field at the first limit point, and the failure site read from it.

Terms used on this page

  • Knockdown factor κ: collapse pressure over the classical pressure of the perfect sphere.
  • Sparse / dense: any two defect centres at least 25° apart (11 to 18 defects), or at least 10° apart (69 to 81 defects). Defects interact when they are close.
  • Failure site: where the buckling deviation (the displacement after removing the uniform contraction) is largest.
  • Site ratio q: amplitude of the second competing site over the first. Clear winner: q < 0.7. Near-tie: q > 0.9.

Benchmark Protocol

Task

From the surface deviation w, predict κ, the displacement field at the peak load and the failure site. Models that read the list of defects instead of the geometry are reported apart.

Evaluation protocols

protocoltrain / val / testwhat changes
B1 within distribution3,299 / 440 / 660nothing (random split, seed 2027), the role of existing benchmarks
B2 deeper defects2,789 / 376 / 1,234test shells have their deepest defect above 2.35 t
B3 sparse to dense1,934 / 265 / 2,200train on sparse shells, test on dense shells, where defects interact

Metrics

  • MRE κ: median relative error of the knockdown factor.
  • Buckling R²: R² of the displacement field after removing each shell's uniform contraction.
  • Clear-site accuracy: share of clear winners whose predicted site lies within 10° of the true one.
  • Near-tie winner rate: on near-ties, share where the model deflects more at the true site than at the second candidate (chance 50%).

Every learned model is the mean of three seeds, with hyperparameters searched on B1 and frozen for B2 and B3. Intervals are shell bootstraps; comparisons are paired.

Results

Accuracy falls from B1 to B3

On B1 the geometry models approach the solver's own resolution. With deeper defects (B2) they keep the field and lose the failure site where defects interact. From sparse to dense (B3) every geometry model falls below the training mean on the field, and the ranking of the models inverts.

Why the predictions degrade

Collapse starts at the deepest defect on all 94 sparse B1 test shells with a clear winner, so a model trained on sparse shells can learn that shortcut. It holds on only 38% of the clear winners among dense shells, because neighbouring defects buckle together. A sharp deflection at the wrong defect adds error twice, so the sharp prediction loses to the smooth mean.

One pair of defects moved closer

Moving one pair of defects from 25° to 10° lowers κ by 14% (median over 100 shells) and moves the failure site between the two defects on 97% of the shells. Models trained on B1 predict changes between −1.1% and +0.3%.

Few-shot adaptation

Five dense shells recover most of the field of FigConvNet and the Transformer; the near-tie winner rate stays between 46% and 52% at every k.

Scope and Future Work

By holding the shell family and load case fixed, BLISS is designed to isolate geometry-induced changes in the instability mechanism, so that changes in model performance can be attributed more directly to geometry. Extending this controlled setting to other structures and loads, and learning the near-tie decision, are the next steps.
